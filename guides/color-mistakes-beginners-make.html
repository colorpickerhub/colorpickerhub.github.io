Color Mistakes Beginners Make

The ten most common color mistakes in web design — and the simple fix for each one.

Using too many colors

The most common beginner mistake is using too many colors. A page with eight bright colors has no hierarchy, because everything shouts at once. The fix is a strict limit: one dominant color, one accent, and neutrals. If you want to add a color, remove one first. Count the colors on your page, including link colors and image borders; if the number surprises you, it is probably too high.

Low-contrast text

Light grey text on white, yellow text on white, and thin colored text on photographs all fail the same way: they look stylish in a mockup and unreadable in real life. The fix is to check every text color with a contrast checker and aim for 4.5 to 1 for body text. If a color fails, darken the text or lighten the background; a small change usually closes the gap without changing the design's character.

Copying colors without context

A color that looks great on another site may fail on yours, because colors depend on their neighbours, the lighting and the content. Copying a palette exactly also makes your brand look like someone else's. Use other palettes as inspiration for structure, such as how many colors and where they appear, then choose your own shades. Your palette should fit your content, not someone else's.

Forgetting color-blind users

Designs that use red and green as the only way to tell success from error exclude about one in twelve men. The fix is cheap: add icons and words alongside color, and choose chart palettes that survive color-blindness simulation. Test your design in greyscale; if the meaning disappears, color is doing too much work alone.

Inconsistent colors across pages

When every page uses a slightly different blue, the site feels amateur even if each page looks fine alone. The fix is a single list of HEX codes that everyone uses, with names and roles. Write it down, share it, and check new pages against it. Consistency is the cheapest way to look professional, and it costs nothing but discipline.

Learning from bad examples

One of the fastest ways to improve your eye for color is to study bad examples. Find a website with clashing colors, low contrast or too many hues, and work out exactly what is wrong and how you would fix it. Would a smaller palette help? Would darker text fix the contrast? This exercise trains your judgement far faster than only looking at good design, because good design hides its decisions while bad design exposes them. Keep a small collection of before-and-after fixes as a personal reference.

Fixing mistakes in an existing site

If your site already has color mistakes, fix them one at a time rather than redesigning everything at once. Start with the worst problem, usually low-contrast text, then reduce the palette to three colors, then check the remaining pages. Small, steady fixes are easier to test and less likely to break something that was working.

Frequently asked questions

What is the biggest color mistake?

Using too many colors with no hierarchy. Limit yourself to one dominant color, one accent, and neutrals.

How do I know if my text contrast is good enough?

Use a contrast checker. Aim for 4.5 to 1 for normal text and 3 to 1 for large text to meet WCAG AA.

Is it okay to copy a palette I like?

Use it as inspiration for structure, but choose your own shades so your brand stays distinct and fits your content.

How do I train my eye for color?

Study both good and bad examples, copy palettes you admire, and test your own choices on real pages with real people.
