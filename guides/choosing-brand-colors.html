Choosing Brand Colors

How to choose brand colors that stand out, work everywhere, and still feel right in five years.

Start from positioning, not taste

Brand colors should express what makes the business different. A budget airline and a luxury hotel should not share a palette, even if the founder likes the same blue. Write a short positioning sentence: who you serve, what you promise, and how you differ from rivals. Then list three adjectives for the brand, such as bold, careful or playful, and judge every color against those words. Personal taste matters, but it comes second to positioning.

Check the competitive landscape

Collect the main colors of five competitors. If four of them use blue, a blue brand will blend in, however good the shade. Distinctiveness is valuable: a brand that looks different is easier to remember and easier to find again. That does not mean choosing a bizarre color; it means finding a gap, such as a deep green in a sea of blue, and owning it consistently across every touchpoint.

Design for every surface

Brand colors must work on a website, a business card, packaging, a social avatar and a sign. Test your colors at tiny sizes, in one-color black and white, and on cheap printers as well as good screens. A color that only works as a large gradient on a retina display is a weak brand color. Choose colors that survive being photocopied, embroidered or printed on cardboard.

Keep the system small and documented

Most strong brands use one or two main colors plus neutrals. Write them down with HEX, RGB and CMYK values, and note the exact rules: where each color goes, what it never pairs with, and the minimum sizes. A one-page brand color sheet prevents the slow drift where every new designer picks a slightly different blue and the brand quietly falls apart.

Plan for the long term

Trendy colors date fast. A neon shade that feels fresh this year can look tired in two. Prefer colors with depth that you can live with for five years, and let trends live in campaigns and illustrations instead of the logo. If you must rebrand, change the supporting colors first and keep the most recognized one; customers forgive evolution far more easily than a sudden stranger.

Rolling out new brand colors

Once brand colors are chosen, roll them out everywhere at once: website, social profiles, email templates, packaging and signage. A half-finished rollout, where the website uses the new blue but the business cards still use the old one, confuses customers and wastes the investment. Make a checklist of every place the brand appears, update them in one coordinated push, and tell the team when the old colors are officially retired. A clean, complete launch makes the new identity feel deliberate rather than accidental.

When competitors copy your colors

If a competitor copies your brand colors, resist the urge to change yours in a panic. Your customers associate those colors with you, and changing them hands the competitor a victory. Instead, strengthen the rest of your identity: your logo, your voice, your packaging and your service. A brand is more than a color, and customers who know you will not be confused for long.

Frequently asked questions

How many colors should a brand have?

One or two main colors plus neutrals covers most brands. Add an accent only if it has a clear job, such as highlighting offers.

Should my brand color match my industry?

It should fit the feeling of your industry but stand out from direct competitors. Familiar enough to be understood, different enough to be remembered.

Can I change brand colors later?

Yes, but do it gradually and explain the change. Sudden rebrands confuse customers and waste the recognition you built.

How often should a brand change its colors?

Rarely. Strong brands keep their colors for years. Update supporting colors when they feel dated, and change a main color only for a genuine rebrand.
