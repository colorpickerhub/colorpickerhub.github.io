Color Contrast and Accessibility Guide

Make your text readable for everyone. Learn WCAG contrast ratios, how to check them, and how to fix low-contrast designs.

Why contrast matters

Contrast is the difference in brightness between text and its background. Low contrast makes reading slow and tiring for everyone, and impossible for people with low vision. Around one in twelve men has some form of color-vision deficiency, and many more people read screens in bright sunlight, on old monitors, or with tired eyes. Good contrast is not a niche accessibility feature; it is basic readability that benefits every visitor to your site.

Understanding WCAG ratios

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) measure contrast as a ratio from 1 to 1 for identical colors up to 21 to 1 for black on white. Level AA requires 4.5 to 1 for normal text and 3 to 1 for large text, while level AAA requires 7 to 1 and 4.5 to 1. These numbers are not arbitrary: they come from research on what most people can read comfortably. Use our contrast checker to test any pair of colors in seconds.

Common contrast failures

The most common failure is light grey text on a white background, often used for secondary information. It looks elegant in a design mockup and unreadable in real life. Other failures include colored text on colored backgrounds, text over busy photographs, and placeholder text in forms, which is often very faint. Treat placeholder text as decoration only and never put essential instructions in it.

Fixing low contrast without redesigning

You rarely need a new palette. Darken the text color, lighten the background, or add a solid panel behind text that sits on a photo. A semi-transparent dark overlay behind white text over an image fixes most hero sections. For buttons, darken the background until white text passes AA, or switch the button text to a very dark color. Small shifts of ten to twenty percent lightness usually close the gap.

Designing beyond contrast

Contrast is one part of accessible color use. Never use color alone to convey meaning: pair a red error with an icon and words, and a green success state with a tick. Give links an underline as well as a color so they are visible to people who cannot tell the colors apart. Finally, test your finished design with a color-blindness simulator and in greyscale to catch problems that numbers alone miss.

Tools that check contrast for you

You do not need to calculate ratios by hand. Browser developer tools now show contrast ratios directly in the color picker, and our contrast checker on this site tests any pair in seconds. Design tools such as Figma have plugins that scan a whole file and flag every failing pair at once, which is far faster than checking one by one. Build the check into your workflow: run it when you pick a palette, again when you build a component, and once more before launch. Catching a failure early costs minutes; fixing it after launch costs far more.

Frequently asked questions

What contrast ratio do I need for body text?

Aim for at least 4.5 to 1 to meet WCAG AA, the standard most organizations follow. Higher is better for long reading.

Does large text need less contrast?

Yes. WCAG allows 3 to 1 for large text (roughly 18pt or 14pt bold and above) because bigger letters are easier to read.

Is white on black accessible?

Yes, white on black has a ratio of 21 to 1, the maximum. Some people find long passages of reversed text tiring, so use it thoughtfully.

Do I need to test hover and focus states too?

Yes. Every state of a link or button must meet the contrast rules, including hover, focus and disabled states, because users encounter all of them.
