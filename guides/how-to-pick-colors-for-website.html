How to Pick Colors for a Website

A practical step-by-step method for choosing website colors that look professional, stay readable, and match your brand.

Start with the job, not the color

Before you open a color picker, decide what the site must do. A shop needs trust and clear calls to action, a portfolio needs the work to stand out, and a blog needs long comfortable reading. The job tells you whether to lean calm or bold, light or dark, and how much contrast the text needs. Write down one feeling you want visitors to have, such as calm, excited, or confident, and use that as a test for every color you consider. If a color does not support that feeling, it does not belong, however pretty it looks on its own.

Choose one dominant color first

Every good website palette has a leader. Pick a single dominant color for headers, buttons and links, then let everything else support it. A neutral background of white, off-white or very dark grey gives that leader room to breathe. Try your dominant color at large sizes first: a color that looks great as a small swatch can feel overwhelming as a full header. Shrink it to buttons and accents if it is too strong, and let neutrals carry the page.

Add one accent and use it sparingly

An accent color is for the few things that must be noticed: the main button, a price, a warning. Use it on less than ten percent of the page or it stops being an accent and becomes noise. The accent should contrast clearly with your dominant color, so a blue site might use a warm orange or amber for its key button. Keep secondary accents to one at most, and reserve them for success or error states.

Test colors on real text, not swatches

Colors behave differently at text size than in a swatch. Put your body text, headings and buttons on the actual background and read them at arm's length, on a phone, and in bright light. Check your contrast ratio with a checker and aim for at least 4.5 to 1 for body text. Also test your palette in greyscale: if the page still reads clearly, your structure does not depend on color alone, which helps color-blind visitors.

Steal structure, not colors

Look at sites you admire and note how many colors they use and where each one appears, rather than copying their exact shades. Most professional sites use three colors plus neutrals. Save three or four palettes you like, live with them for a day, and then pick the one that still feels right. Finally, write the palette down as HEX codes in a short style guide so every page stays consistent.

Testing with real users

Once you have a shortlist of two or three palettes, test them with real people instead of deciding alone. Show five visitors two versions of the same page and ask which one feels more trustworthy, which button they would click, and what the site seems to sell. Their answers often surprise you: a palette you loved may feel cold to strangers, while a quieter option feels professional. Record the answers, look for patterns rather than single opinions, and let the majority guide the final choice. Testing does not need to be formal or expensive; even a quick poll among friends or colleagues reveals problems you cannot see yourself, because you are too close to the design to judge it fresh.

Frequently asked questions

How many colors should a website use?

Three colors plus neutrals is a safe rule: one dominant, one accent, and one supporting color, with white, grey or black for backgrounds and text.

Should I use a dark or light theme?

Light themes suit long reading and shops; dark themes suit media, dashboards and evening use. Either works if contrast is strong and text stays readable.

Where do I start if I have no brand color?

Start with your audience and feeling, try a palette generator with one color you like, and test the result on a real page before committing.

How long should I spend choosing colors?

A few hours of focused work is usually enough. Shortlist two or three palettes, test them on a real page with real people, and pick the winner. Endless tweaking rarely improves the result.
