Gradient Colors: A Practical Guide

From subtle fades to bold multi-color blends: how to design gradients that look modern, not dated.

What makes a good gradient

A gradient is a smooth transition between two or more colors. Good gradients feel intentional: the colors relate to each other, the transition is smooth, and the gradient has a job, such as adding depth to a hero section or making a button feel tactile. Bad gradients feel accidental: unrelated colors, harsh bands, or a rainbow that fights the content. The difference is usually restraint in the number of colors and care in the transition.

Choosing gradient colors

The easiest good gradients use colors that are close on the wheel, such as blue to purple, or two shades of one hue. These always look smooth. More daring gradients pair complementary colors, such as orange to pink, which can look stunning in a hero image but tiring across a whole page. Avoid gradients that pass through muddy middle colors: a red-to-green gradient passes through brown, which looks dirty. Test the midpoint and adjust the colors until it stays clean.

Linear, radial and angled

Linear gradients run in a straight line, most often top to bottom or diagonally, and suit backgrounds and cards. Radial gradients spread from a center point and suit glows, spotlights and soft highlights. The angle matters: a diagonal gradient feels more dynamic than a vertical one, and a subtle gradient at a shallow angle can add depth without shouting. Keep the angle consistent across a design so the light feels like it comes from one direction.

Using gradients with text

Text over a gradient is risky, because one end of the gradient may not contrast enough with the text. Either keep the gradient subtle, add a dark overlay, or place the text on a solid panel over the gradient. Gradient text, where the letters themselves are filled with a gradient, works for short headings and logos but not for body copy. Always re-check contrast at both ends of the gradient before shipping.

When gradients date a design

Heavy, glossy gradients were a hallmark of early smartphone design and can look dated when overused. Modern gradients tend to be softer, with less contrast between stops, or used in small doses such as buttons and illustrations. If your gradient feels like a 2012 app icon, try reducing the contrast between the two colors, adding a third stop in the middle, or using the gradient only as an accent rather than a background.

Gradients in logos and branding

Gradients in logos are tempting, because they look modern and vivid, but they are hard to reproduce: a gradient logo needs a full-color version, a one-color version for fax and embroidery, and a version that works on dark backgrounds. Many famous gradient logos, such as the one used by a well-known photo app, work because the gradient is simple and the shape is bold. If you use a gradient in a logo, keep it to two colors, test it in one color, and make sure the brand is still recognizable without it.

Frequently asked questions

How many colors should a gradient have?

Two is safest, three works for a richer blend. More than three usually looks messy unless the colors are closely related.

Why does my gradient look banded?

Banding happens when there are not enough shades between stops. Add a middle stop, or use a slight noise texture to smooth it.

Can I use gradients for buttons?

Yes. A subtle gradient can make a button feel tactile. Keep the contrast low and the text readable.

Do gradients work in logos?

They can, if the gradient is simple and the shape is bold. Always design a one-color version too, for printing and embroidery.
