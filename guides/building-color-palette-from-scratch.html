Building a Color Palette from Scratch

Learn how to build a balanced color palette from a single starting color, with harmony rules and practical checks.

Begin with one color you love

A palette starts with a single seed color. It might come from a logo, a photo, a product, or simply a shade you keep returning to. Test the seed at different sizes and on light and dark backgrounds, because a color that works as a button may fail as a page background. If you have no seed, pick from a photo you like: sample the dominant color of a landscape or product shot and use that as your starting point.

Apply a harmony rule

Harmony rules are shortcuts for colors that look good together. Complementary pairs sit opposite each other on the color wheel and give strong contrast. Analogous colors sit side by side and feel calm and natural. Triadic schemes use three colors spaced evenly around the wheel and feel lively but balanced. Our palette generator applies these rules for you; try each one on your seed and see which mood fits your project.

Add neutrals and shades

A palette of only bright colors is exhausting. Add two or three neutrals, such as a near-white background, a mid grey for secondary text, and a near-black for headings. Then create lighter and darker versions of your main colors by adjusting lightness in HSL. These shades give you colors for hover states, borders, cards and subtle backgrounds without introducing new hues.

Assign a role to every color

Write down what each color is for: primary buttons, links, success messages, warnings, backgrounds, text. A color without a role gets used randomly and the design drifts. Aim for five to seven named roles in total. When a new screen needs a color, the role list tells you which one to use, and that consistency is what makes a product feel professionally designed rather than assembled.

Test the palette in context

Build one real screen with the palette before finalising it: a header, a paragraph, a button, a form and an error message. Check it on a phone and a desktop, in daylight, and in greyscale. Ask two people what feeling the screen gives them. If the palette survives a real screen, it will survive the whole project; if it fails there, adjust now rather than after twenty pages are built.

Documenting the finished palette

When the palette is done, write it down properly. A good palette document lists every color with its HEX, RGB and HSL values, a name, and the role it plays, such as primary button or error text. Add a small swatch for each one and an example of it in use, so a new team member can use the palette without asking questions. Store the document where the team actually works, in the design file or the project wiki, and update it in one place when a color changes. A palette that lives only in one person's head will drift within months.

Frequently asked questions

How many colors should a palette have?

Five to seven colors including neutrals is a good size. Fewer feels limited, more becomes hard to use consistently.

Can I build a palette from a photo?

Yes. Sample the dominant colors from a photo you like, then adjust their lightness and saturation so they work as interface colors.

What if my palette looks dull?

Try raising saturation slightly on the accent color only, or add one warm accent to a cool palette. Small changes have a big effect.

Should I name my palette colors?

Yes. Names like Ocean, Coral or Mist are easier to discuss than HEX codes, and naming each color by its role prevents confusion in the team.
