Picking Colors for Dark Mode

Dark mode is not just inverted light mode. Learn how to choose dark backgrounds, readable text, and accents that glow.

Why dark mode needs its own palette

Simply inverting a light palette produces harsh, glowing colors and muddy greys. Dark mode needs colors designed for dark backgrounds: desaturated accents, softer whites and layered greys. The reason is optical: a bright color on a dark background appears more intense than the same color on white, so colors that were calm in light mode can shout in dark mode. Design the dark palette separately, even if it shares the same hues.

Choosing the background

Pure black backgrounds cause eye strain and make shadows invisible. Most good dark modes use a very dark grey or a dark version of the brand color, such as #121212 or a deep navy. Then layer surfaces: a slightly lighter grey for cards, and a lighter still for menus and dialogs. These small steps in lightness create hierarchy without needing borders everywhere, and they keep the design feeling deep rather than flat.

Text colors in dark mode

Pure white text on a dark background can glow and cause halation, where letters seem to blur at the edges. Use an off-white, around #E0E0E0, for main text, and a mid grey for secondary text. Check contrast carefully: grey text that passed on white often fails on dark backgrounds, so re-test every combination with a contrast checker rather than assuming the light-mode values still hold.

Adjusting accent colors

Reduce the saturation of your accent colors by ten to twenty percent for dark mode, and raise their lightness slightly so they stay visible. A vivid blue that worked on white may need to become a softer sky blue on dark grey. Buttons often need a lighter fill or an outline style in dark mode, because a dark button on a dark background disappears. Test every interactive state, including hover and focus, in the dark theme.

Testing dark mode properly

Test dark mode in a dark room and in daylight, on OLED and LCD screens, and with the system setting toggled both ways. Check images and illustrations too: a logo designed for white may need a light version, and photos may need a subtle border so they do not blend into the background. Finally, let users choose: respect the system preference by default, but always offer a manual switch.

Dark mode and battery life

On phones with OLED screens, dark mode can save battery, because black pixels on an OLED display are effectively switched off and use almost no power. The saving is real but modest, and it depends on how much of the screen is truly dark: a dark grey background saves less than a pure black one, though pure black is harder on the eyes. Do not choose dark mode for battery reasons alone; choose it because your users prefer it, and let the system setting decide the default. The battery benefit is a pleasant bonus, not a design reason.

Frequently asked questions

Is pure black a good dark mode background?

Usually not. Very dark grey (#121212 or similar) is easier on the eyes and lets you layer surfaces with slightly lighter greys.

Do I need separate colors for dark mode?

Yes, at least for accents and text. The same hues can work, but their saturation and lightness usually need adjusting.

Should dark mode be the default?

Follow the user's system setting by default, and always offer a manual toggle. Some people strongly prefer one or the other.

Does dark mode really save battery?

On OLED screens, yes, noticeably. On LCD screens, barely at all, because the backlight stays on regardless of the pixels.
