Using a Color Picker Tool Effectively

A color picker is more than a pretty wheel. Learn how to sample colors, save palettes, and get exact values every time.

Picking from the wheel vs typing values

Most pickers offer two ways in: a visual wheel or swatch, and a text field for a HEX or RGB value. The wheel is best for exploring, the text field is best for precision. When a brand guide gives you a HEX code, type it in rather than trying to match it by eye; screens and eyes both lie. When you are exploring, drag slowly and watch the preview at large size, because a color that looks right in a tiny square can surprise you when it fills a header.

Sampling colors from images

Many pickers let you sample a color from a photo or screenshot. This is the fastest way to build a palette from a brand photo or a mood board. Sample the largest area for your dominant color, a bright detail for your accent, and a shadow for a dark neutral. Sample three or four points rather than one, because photos contain gradients and the average is often muddy. Always check the sampled color on a plain background before using it.

Using sliders with intent

RGB sliders are precise but hard to think with; HSL sliders match how we describe color. To lighten a color, raise lightness; to soften it, lower saturation; to shift its mood, move the hue a few degrees. Change one slider at a time and watch the result. A common workflow is to find a hue you like, set saturation to a comfortable level, then create a family of shades by copying the color and changing lightness only.

Saving and sharing palettes

A palette you cannot find again is useless. Save your colors as HEX codes in a note, a design file, or a shared style guide, with a name for each one. When sharing with a team, include the role of each color alongside its code, so a developer knows which one is the button color and which is the error color. Keep one canonical list and update it in one place when a color changes.

Checking your picks before shipping

Before you commit, test your picked colors in the real context: on the actual page, on a phone, and with the contrast checker. Print the page in greyscale to check that structure survives without color. Ask a colleague to describe the feeling in three words. If their words match your intention and the numbers pass the checks, the picker has done its job.

Picking colors for print vs screen

Colors picked on a screen do not always print the same way, because screens use light and printers use ink. A vivid blue that glows on a monitor can print as a dull purple, and a bright yellow can disappear on cheap paper. If your design will be printed, check the CMYK values of your colors and print a test page before committing. Keep a note of which colors are screen-only and which work in print, and choose brand colors that survive both, so the website and the business card look like they belong to the same company.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my picked color look different on another screen?

Screens vary in brightness, calibration and color profile. Test on more than one device and accept small differences.

Should I use the eyedropper or type HEX codes?

Type HEX codes for brand colors and exact matches. Use the eyedropper for exploring and sampling from photos.

How do I save a palette?

Write down the HEX codes with a name and role for each color. Keep the list in your design file or style guide.

Why do printed colors look different?

Screens mix red, green and blue light, while printers mix cyan, magenta, yellow and black ink. Some screen colors simply cannot be reproduced in ink.
